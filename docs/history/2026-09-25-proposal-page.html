A live map for the Anti-Debate

From Benjamin Life (@omniharmonic), for Stephanie Lepp Draft · your answers shape the build

Two people talk. As they do, a quiet map grows behind them: the claims each person makes, the roots those claims grow from, the ground they share, and the higher ground that neither held alone. It is built on the work that grew out of your approach, first as Dialectical Topology, then Ontography. This page shows how it would work live, and asks you what it should and shouldn't do.

How it works

Four moves, with a person in the loop

Listen

Who said what

Open-source speech recognition (NVIDIA Parakeet) runs on a laptop in the room. No audio leaves the venue. It works in person, on Zoom, or from a livestream.

Map

Claims, as they're made

Each finished turn becomes a few short claims, each linked to the exact words said. Claims connect to earlier ones: builds on, challenges, agrees.

Deepen

Roots and bridges

Every minute or so it looks beneath the claims for the assumptions they rest on, the words used in different senses, and the one disagreement the others depend on.

Reveal

You choose what shows

Nothing reaches the screen without a person approving it. You can show the map continuously, only between rounds, or keep it to yourself.

Getting the speaker right, every time

Everything on the map depends on knowing who said it. Putting one person's words under the other's name, on stage, is the worst mistake this tool could make. So identifying speakers is its own layer, running in every setup, even when each person has their own mic.

Separate mics still pick up each other's voices. People overlap. A moderator or audience member joins in. On a livestream, everyone arrives on one channel. The system combines several signals and asks a person whenever it isn't sure.

  • 1Which mic is loudest. Drops the faint copy of a voice that bleeds into the other person's mic.
  • 2Voice separation. A speaker-separation model (Sortformer or pyannote) tracks who is speaking, including when two people talk at once.
  • 3A 30-second voice sample at sound check. Names each voice. Anyone else becomes "audience" and never lands in a debater's column.
  • 4A confidence score. When the signals agree, the line goes straight through. When they don't, it waits.
  • 5A person decides. The operator confirms unsure lines with one key and can fix any line later. The claims move with it.
What it asks of the event

A light footprint in the room

A mic per speakerLapel or handheld, into a small audio interface or the venue's mixer. On Zoom, each person on their own device.
Thirty seconds at sound checkEach speaker says a few sentences so the system learns their voice.
One operator with a laptopBenjamin or a volunteer, approving what goes on screen and confirming unsure speakers.
A screen, if you want oneA projector or stream overlay. Or none, if the map is for you alone.

What it will not do

  • No scoring. No winner, no steelman score, no leaderboard.
  • No forced synthesis. Where two views stay different, the map says so plainly.
  • No words put in anyone's mouth. Every claim links to what was actually said. Inferred roots are marked as guesses. Debaters can correct anything, live or afterwards.
  • No upstaging. The people on stage are the event. The map supports them.
